Master's Thesis · Federated Learning Security · NTUT

The Defense That Says Where It Breaks

Federated learning trains one shared model across devices that never hand over their data. That privacy guarantee is also the attack surface: the server sees model updates, not the data behind them, so a client that poisons its updates can steer the global model and the server has no ground truth to check it against. My thesis proposes L-MAD, a defense sized for the resource budget of an edge device, and then spends its final chapter establishing exactly when it fails.

My role: sole author, advised by Shiang-Jiun Chen, Ph.D., in the Software Security Lab at National Taipei University of Technology. The method, the implementation, all six reimplemented baselines, the experiment campaign, and the 100-page write-up. Defended July 2026.

  • 97.5% DDoS-class F₁ under a 30% poisoning attack
  • +25.7 points over the strongest baseline
  • 2.6× faster than the nearest layer-wise defense
  • 6 competing defenses reimplemented
  • 21 experiment groups, 20 seeds
  • 0 rows of auxiliary data on the server
A note on scope: this page is a summary written for a general software audience, not a substitute for the thesis. The bar and scatter charts are redrawn from the document's own results so they follow this site's light and dark themes; the system diagrams and the attack-success panels are the thesis figures as they appear in it. Nothing has been rescaled or reframed. The numbers are the deposited ones.

The Problem

Federated learning has one structural weakness, and it follows directly from its main feature. The standard aggregation rule, Federated Averaging, is a weighted mean of the clients' updates, and a mean has a breakdown point of zero: a single update, made extreme enough, drags the result anywhere the attacker wants. Because the server never sees the underlying data, it cannot tell a poisoned update from an unusual but honest one by inspection.

Defenses exist. At the resource-constrained edge, each family pays a price that undercuts the reason for deploying there in the first place:

  1. Distance-based rules (Krum)Compare every client against every other, an O(K²d) cost that grows quadratically with federation size and model dimension
  2. Validation-based rules (FLTrust)Score updates against a clean dataset held on the server, which relaxes the data-absence premise that motivated federating at all
  3. Statistical layer-wise rules (LGP)Add clustering and per-layer feature extraction on top, the most expensive option of the three

There is also a fourth deficiency, orthogonal to the other three and shared by all of them: they reject whole clients. In a targeted label-flipping attack the adversary may corrupt only the classification layers while the early feature-extraction layers keep learning something real. Throwing away the client throws that away too, along with the battery and bandwidth the device already spent producing it.

What L-MAD Does

Two stages, neither of which needs any data on the server.

Stage 1: a cross-round reliability pre-pass

Each round, every client's update is scored for directional agreement with a robust consensus direction. Disagreement accumulates into a per-client distrust score that decays over time, so one atypical round is forgiven and a pattern is not. Once the anomalies persist, the client is quarantined permanently. This is the stage that catches the patient attacker, the one who is unremarkable in any single round and only looks wrong across many. The entire memory cost is one scalar per client.

Stage 2: a per-layer MAD gate

Survivors are then compared layer by layer against the population median for that layer, with the tolerance set by the median absolute deviation. MAD has a 50% breakdown point, the highest any location estimator can attain, against the mean's 0%. A layer that exceeds the threshold is dropped on its own, and the client's remaining layers are aggregated normally.

Three choices that keep it edge-viable

The method only matters if it runs inside the budget. Layer profiling means the gate is applied only to high-risk decision layers rather than every tensor. The variance-driven threshold is capped, so heterogeneous but honest data cannot widen the tolerance until the gate stops rejecting anything. And the exact element-wise median has a chunked, memory-bounded approximation behind it for when the model does not fit the budget. Server state stays constant per client regardless of model size.

The system it runs inside

None of the above is a paper result until something runs it. The harness is four subsystems: storage for the raw dataset and the experiment metrics, a preprocessing and model layer, the edge clients that train and hand back parameters, and the server that profiles layers, aggregates and evaluates. Every experiment in this write-up came out of it.

Results

The reference threat model is a 30% targeted label-flipping attack, three of ten clients relabeling normal traffic as attack traffic, on DNN-EdgeIIoT. Because the classes are imbalanced roughly 4.8 to 1, the primary metric is F₁ on the DDoS class rather than accuracy. Attack success rate is the fraction of normal traffic the poisoned model misclassifies, so lower is better.

L-MAD holds 97.52%, statistically indistinguishable from its own clean-data performance of 97.11%, meaning the attack costs it almost nothing. No baseline clears 72%. The mechanism behind the gap is detection recall: L-MAD catches 93.4% of poisoned contributions, against 85.6% for LGP and 20.6% for Krum, whose weakness here is instructive. A label-flipping update is geometrically central rather than an outlier, so a distance-based rule rarely picks it as the thing to discard.

The most telling comparison is ATTA-FL-Lite. It is the only baseline granted a clean validation set on the server, and its whole-client verdicts still trail the per-layer defense by more than 31 points. Extra information did not substitute for granularity.

Cost against robustness

Robustness that misses the resource budget is not an edge defense. Against LGP, its only layer-wise peer, there is no trade-off to argue about: L-MAD is about 2.6× faster and far more robust. Against the cheap traditional rules the trade-off is real but small, roughly 18 ms per round over Krum in exchange for 25.7 points of F₁.

How I Tried to Break It

The headline number took one run. Establishing what it was worth took the rest of the year, and it is the part of this project closest to the testing work I do elsewhere.

Reimplement the competition instead of quoting it

Citing a paper's published number compares your tuned method against their default configuration on their dataset. All six baselines were rebuilt in the same harness, and the closest competitor got its own parameter sweep so the comparison ran against its best configuration rather than its first. Two of the baseline implementations carry their own pytest suites, alongside tests for the data loader, the label-flip transform, and the model, because a quiet bug in a competitor would have manufactured my result for me.

The pipeline the numbers come out of

A robustness claim is only as good as the data path under it, so both halves of that path are fixed before any strategy runs. Preprocessing is deterministic and fitted on the training split alone. Partitioning is where the heterogeneity lives: a Dirichlet allocation that decides how unevenly the labels are spread across clients, which is the axis the sweep below moves.

Sweep the axes, not just the reference point

Twenty-one experiment groups cover attacker ratio, attack intensity, the detection threshold, Dirichlet heterogeneity, federation size out to 100 clients, four attack families, and component ablations that remove each mechanism in turn to confirm it is carrying weight. A defense characterized at one operating point is a defense nobody knows how to deploy.

Twenty seeds, because three is an anecdote

Every baseline's three-seed mean turned out to be hiding a partition where it collapsed entirely. Running twenty data partitions exposed the same property in my own method: it fails on 40% of them. Reporting that required Wilson confidence intervals on the failure rate and paired significance tests, McNemar and Wilcoxon, rather than eyeballing bar charts. The tests place L-MAD above undefended FedAvg with p = 0.003, and not separably from LGP, BRAFL, or ATTA-FL-Lite at p ≥ 0.06. That second finding is in the thesis at the same size as the first.

Design the experiment that would embarrass you

Stage 1 over-convicts honest clients, and the obvious rebuttal is that the threshold is simply too aggressive. So I ran a sixteen-configuration sweep to find the softer setting that would prove it. There isn't one: every reduction in the honest false-positive rate weakened Byzantine robustness in lock-step, and only the aggressive default held attack success near zero. The cost is intrinsic to the directional criterion rather than a tuning mistake. That is a negative result about my own method, and it is in the document because the alternative is letting a reader discover it later.

Where It Breaks

Chapter 5 lists five limitations by name. Stating the envelope is the price of asking anyone to deploy this, so here it is.

  1. It over-convicts honest clients. The directional criterion cannot separate an honestly divergent update from an adversarial one. At the reference point it convicts eight of ten honest clients. Utility survives because rejection falls back to the robust median rather than deleting the contribution, but the mechanism is blunt and the sweep above says it cannot be softened for free.
  2. It holds to three attackers in ten, and it is partition-dependent. Robustness fails on 40% of twenty partitions (Wilson 95% CI, 22 to 61%). That is the lowest failure rate among the five comparable methods, which makes it the most reliable of them rather than a reliable one. No median-based defense survives 50% attackers, by construction.
  3. It trails on one attack family. Leading on directional and random poisoning, it loses to LGP on the scaling attack, where a boosted update slips through profiler-exempt layers before the reliability register convicts.
  4. The advantage narrows as the federation grows. From clear dominance at ten clients to parity with undefended FedAvg at fifty, as per-device data thins. An optional loss probe holds it near 97% through a hundred clients, but only by reintroducing the trusted root dataset the method was designed to avoid, which is a different method with a different premise.
  5. The per-layer claim is under-tested on this model. The compact tabular network has only two tensors that reach the gate, so the layer-wise-versus-whole-client distinction is thin here. The salvage analysis found the gate distinguishing only transiently and almost always on honest clients, 23 of 24 events, and over twenty partitions it did not lower the failure rate. Its demonstrated scope is one adverse seed, not a general gain, and proving the idea properly needs a deeper architecture.

What I'd Do Next

The limitations are the roadmap. Adding a profiling category for representation-extraction layers would carry the method to CNNs and Transformers, which is also the only setting where the per-layer contribution can actually be tested at scale. The threat model should broaden to adversaries jointly optimizing against both gates, since the scaling attack already shows where that gap is. The loss probe needs folding into the core method without its trusted-root dependency, via cross-client agreement or a held-out split, so that one discriminator handles both the heterogeneity and the scaling triggers. And with all evidence from a single dataset and a binary task, replication elsewhere is what would turn a characterized result into a general one.

The decisive test is hardware. Everything here is simulation, and a constant-memory design earns its keep on a real constrained device or nowhere.

Key Engineering Learnings

  1. A result you have not tried to break is not a result. One run produced 97.5%. Twenty groups of runs produced the sentence that says what 97.5% is worth, and only the second one is usable by somebody else.
  2. Rebuild the comparison, don't cite it. Quoting published numbers silently compares your best effort against someone else's defaults. Reimplementing all six baselines, and tuning the nearest one, is the entire reason the 25-point gap means anything.
  3. Three seeds is an anecdote. Every mean in the field hid a partition where the method collapsed, including mine. The twenty-seed study is the part of the evaluation I would keep if I had to throw out the rest.
  4. Run the experiment designed to embarrass you. The sixteen-configuration sweep existed to test whether my method's worst property was just a bad default. It wasn't, and finding that out myself was much cheaper than having a committee find it.
  5. Bound the claim. "It works" is not a finding. "Holds to three of ten attackers, at federations near ten, against directional and random poisoning, and here is what happens outside that" is a finding, and it is the version that survives contact with a reviewer.
