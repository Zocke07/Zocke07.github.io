The Process Was Fine. The Output Still Fell.

Our graduation capstone set out to document how a commercial test automation team actually works, gate by gate, from picking a requirement ticket to a pull request the company's own engineers agree to merge. Then we counted four years of that team's output and found it had dropped by roughly two thirds from its peak, with the process unchanged throughout. The pipeline turned out to be the least interesting finding. The interesting one is that a healthy pipeline and falling numbers are perfectly compatible.

My role: one of three students on the capstone, embedded in the team as an industry-academia co-op for the eighteen months the study was written across. The report is joint work by the three of us, advised by Shiang-Jiun Chen, Ph.D. The practice it documents belongs to the company's engineers, not to us.

  • 4 years of test-case output analysed
  • 18 months embedded in the team
  • 10–20 hours per week of development
  • Every 2 weeks a demo to the company
  • 7 gates from ticket to merge
  • 3 students, one advisor

A note on confidentiality: this write-up respects my NDA. The product, its internal ticket identifiers, and its business logic are omitted or generalized, and nothing here reproduces the capstone report itself. What remains is the engineering process and the shape of the data, both of which are ordinary industry practice.

The Question

There is no shortage of writing about test automation best practice. Almost all of it is descriptive: here is what a disciplined team does, here is the gate it should hold, here is why flaky tests are expensive. Very little of it goes on to measure a team that is already doing those things and ask whether it shows.

We had access that made the second question answerable. Three of us were inside the team rather than observing it, working the same sprints as the company's engineers on an enterprise data-center infrastructure management platform. So the capstone asked two things instead of one. What does this pipeline actually require of a person, gate by gate? And when you count what comes out the other end over several years, what do you see?

The first question produced a description. The second produced a problem, which is the more useful outcome and was not the one we expected.

What the Pipeline Actually Requires

Seven gates stand between picking up a requirement ticket and having a test case counted as finished. None of them is unusual on its own. The discipline is that none of them is skippable, including the two that feel like overhead until the first time they save you.

  1. Select the caseAt sprint planning, pull cases from the backlog and estimate difficulty and hours for each.
  2. Design the steps by hand, then get them signed offRefine the steps from the requirement document, run them manually to find logical holes, then send the written steps to the product manager for confirmation. No code exists yet.
  3. Implement as keywords, togetherMob programming: the whole team on one case at once. Steps become reusable keywords under the team's naming and duplication rules, then a lint pass.
  4. Prove it in CITen runs at normal network speed, then five more deliberately throttled, so the case has to survive a slow environment as well as a fast one.
  5. Code review and sprint reviewCheck the code against the house style and, separately, demo the sprint's results to the person responsible.
  6. Open the pull requestBranch merged toward main only through a PR.
  7. Fix until the engineers approveThe company's engineers review and comment. The case is not finished when it passes. It is finished when they merge it.

The same seven gates as our report drew them, shaded by the tool each stage lives in. What the flowchart shows that the list above cannot is the loops: a rejected review returns to Modify rather than to the start, and sprint-review suggestions re-enter at case refinement. Nothing here is a straight line to done.

Gate two is the one worth stopping on. Writing the test steps in prose, testing them by hand, and getting a product manager to agree before any code is written feels like the slowest possible start. It is also the only gate that catches a misunderstanding while it is still a paragraph rather than a suite.

This page is about the process and what came out of it. For how the suites themselves are built so that hundreds of them can run at once against one live server, that is the other case study.

What Four Years of Data Showed

We pulled the team's test-case records for four consecutive September-to-September years and counted two things: cases created, and cases merged. The pipeline above did not change across any of them.

Test cases created Test cases merged

Output peaked in the second year at around eighty cases created, then fell for two years running to under thirty. That is roughly a two-thirds decline from the peak, and the final year sits barely above the first year's ramp-up.

The report attributes the decline to three causes, and the notable thing about all three is where they sit. Requirements grew more complex, so a single case took longer to cover comprehensively. Product versions were unstable, so effort that would have gone into new cases went into reworking existing ones that the build had broken. And communication across functional teams drifted, so expectations and requirements came apart and coverage was left incomplete.

Not one of them is "the team got worse." Every cause is upstream of the people being measured, which is the part that makes the number misleading if you read it alone.

One detail in the chart supports that reading. In the last two years, cases merged came out above cases created. A team that has stopped working does not close more than it opens. What that shape suggests is a team draining work-in-progress and rework rather than starting new coverage, which is exactly what the three causes would predict.

What the Numbers Cannot Settle

The finding is worth stating plainly, and so is its weakness. A count of test cases is a proxy for output, not for value. A case covering a hard cross-subsystem interaction and a case covering one dropdown both count as one. If the later years' cases were individually harder, which the first cause implies they were, then the fall in the count overstates the fall in work done, and we have no measure that would separate the two.

The causes are the team's own account rather than an experiment. We did not vary anything and observe an effect; we asked why, listened, and found the explanation consistent with the shape of the data. That is a reasonable thing to publish as long as it is labelled, and it is not the same as having demonstrated it.

And we were present for the last of the four years, not the first three. The early data is a record we inherited and read, not one we watched being made.

The Exhibit We Had to Rebuild

A capstone is supposed to be demonstrable, and ours documented work that cannot leave the company. Every suite we wrote, every keyword, and every ticket it maps to sits behind the NDA. Showing the actual artefact was never an option.

So the exhibit is a reconstruction. I built a small web application and a set of Robot Framework suites against it, deliberately walking the same seven gates on a target that could be published: steps designed and written down first, keywords composed rather than duplicated, a lint pass, repeat runs before merge.

It demonstrates the pipeline honestly and it demonstrates nothing about scale. The real platform's difficulty is hundreds of suites contending for one live server, and a purpose-built demo app has none of that. It is a faithful model of the process and a poor model of the problem, and it is worth being clear about which.

Key Engineering Learnings

  1. A process metric is not a productivity metric. Cases per year measures how much got made, and says nothing about how hard each one was. Reported on its own to someone who was not there, it describes a team in decline. All three actual causes were outside the team's control.
  2. Look upstream when output falls. Complexity growth, unstable builds, and cross-team misalignment all arrive from somewhere else and land on the people holding the last gate. The team is where the number is measured, not where it is decided.
  3. The slowest gate paid for itself. Writing test steps in prose, running them by hand, and getting the product manager to agree before any code exists is the least appealing part of the pipeline. It is also the only place a misunderstanding is still cheap.
  4. Finished is defined by someone else. A case that passes CI is not done. It is done when the company's engineers approve the pull request, which moves the definition of finished out of the author's hands, where it belongs.
  5. Measuring your own team is uncomfortable and worth doing anyway. The easy capstone was the description we set out to write. The one that taught us something required publishing a number that did not flatter the group we were part of, and then being careful about what it did and did not prove.
